If your rabbit has not eaten for twelve hours, that is not a fussy day. It is an emergency, and it is the single most common way pet rabbits die.

This is the one thing every rabbit owner should know, and it is not obvious. A dog that skips a meal is usually fine by morning. A rabbit that skips a meal may be hours into something that kills it.

## Why a rabbit is different

A rabbit's digestive system is built to have food moving through it almost constantly. It is not designed to sit idle. When a rabbit stops eating, the gut slows and then stops — a condition usually called gut stasis, or ileus.

Once it stops, the problem feeds itself. Gas builds up, which is painful. Pain makes the rabbit less likely to eat. Not eating slows the gut further. A rabbit can go from slightly quiet to critically ill inside a day, and by the time it looks obviously unwell you have already lost most of your margin.

The important part: gut stasis is often a symptom, not the original problem. Dental pain, a change of diet, stress, or something else entirely can start it. But the stasis itself becomes the thing that is most likely to kill the animal, whatever began it.

## The two things to check

You do not need equipment and you do not need to be an expert. You need to know two facts about the last twelve hours.

- Has the rabbit eaten anything at all?
- Are there fresh droppings, in the normal number and the normal size?

That is it. Those two together are the most sensitive early warning available to a rabbit owner, and both are visible from across the room.

Droppings are the more useful of the two, because they tell you about the gut rather than about your rabbit's mood. Rabbits produce a lot of them — a healthy rabbit leaves a great many round, dry, evenly sized droppings every day. What matters is the change.

Watch for droppings that are:

- Absent entirely
- Much smaller than usual
- Misshapen, or strung together with fur
- Soft, or smeared

Smaller droppings usually mean less food is going through. That is the earliest signal you will get, and it often appears before the rabbit looks unwell at all.

## What counts as an emergency

Call a vet the same day if your rabbit has not eaten for around twelve hours, or if droppings have stopped or become very small.

Treat it as urgent, out of hours if necessary, if you also see any of the following:

- Sitting hunched, pressing the belly toward the floor
- Grinding teeth loudly, which in rabbits signals pain rather than contentment
- Not moving much, or not responding to food it normally loves
- A belly that feels tight, bloated or hard

A rabbit in this state needs pain relief, fluids and often gut motility drugs. This is not something to manage at home, and it is not something that improves with waiting.

## Find a rabbit vet before you need one

Not every small animal practice is confident with rabbits. Rabbits are not small cats — their anaesthetic risk, their drug tolerances and their dentistry are genuinely different, and experience matters.

The time to find out who your nearest rabbit-experienced vet is, and what they do out of hours, is a quiet Tuesday. Not at ten at night with a hunched rabbit in a carrier.

Write the number down somewhere you will find it under stress.

## The boring prevention that actually works

Most gut stasis traces back to diet, and the fix is unglamorous.

- Unlimited good hay, all the time. It should be the overwhelming majority of what the rabbit eats. It keeps the gut moving and it wears the teeth down.
- A limited, measured amount of pellets. Free-feeding pellets is one of the most common causes of a rabbit eating too little hay.
- Fresh water always available.
- Fresh greens daily, introduced gradually rather than all at once.

Rabbit teeth grow continuously throughout life. If they wear unevenly, chewing becomes painful, the rabbit eats less, and the gut slows — the same cascade, arriving by a different road. Hay is what does most of that wearing, which is why it matters more than anything else in the bowl.

## Why writing it down changes things

The hardest part of all of this is that it depends on noticing a change, and change is exactly what people are worst at seeing day to day. Nobody remembers with confidence whether yesterday's droppings were smaller. You remember how your rabbit is today, and today always feels normal.

A record fixes that. Ten seconds a day — did they eat, were the droppings normal — turns a vague feeling into something you can actually look at. And when you do end up at the vet, "she has eaten less on four of the last six days and the droppings got smaller on Tuesday" is worth considerably more than "she has seemed a bit off lately".

That is the whole reason Petvity asks rabbit owners those two questions first, before anything else. For a rabbit, they are the two that matter most.

## The short version

- A rabbit that has not eaten for twelve hours needs a vet, not an overnight wait
- Droppings that stop or shrink are the earliest warning you will get
- Teeth grinding and a hunched posture mean pain, and mean go now
- Unlimited hay prevents more of this than anything else you can do
- Find a rabbit-experienced vet before there is an emergency

This article is general information and is not a substitute for veterinary advice. If you are worried about your rabbit right now, ring a vet.
